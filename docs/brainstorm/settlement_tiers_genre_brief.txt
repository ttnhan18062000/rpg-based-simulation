Genre Brief: Settlements Below the City — Camps, Lairs & Ruins
================================================================================

This is a genre/craft review, not a code-consistency check like the other
briefs in this folder. You have no access to the codebase and don't need
one — everything you need to evaluate is below. Read this the way you'd
read a design document for a tabletop RPG, a fantasy novel's worldbuilding
bible, or a game's bestiary/dungeon-design pass. The question isn't "does
this match the engine" (already verified separately) — it's "does this
feel like a real fantasy world, judged against what books, tabletop RPGs,
and games in this genre actually do with goblin warrens, monster lairs,
and ruins." We want a different kind of eye on it than the technical
reviews this project usually runs.

Background, brief version
-----------------------------
This is an ongoing simulation project with a "layer model" — Entity (one
character) sits inside City (a settlement) sits inside Region (biome/
geography), with Country (political power), Clan (cross-border
organizations), and Race (a people, collectively) as separate,
cross-cutting dimensions rather than one strict containment ladder. City
was recently confirmed to already exist as real data (a settlement region
with buildings and population) but conflated with Region as a single
class — since fixed. If you want the full technical history, it's in this
same folder as layer_model_discussion_brief.txt and
layer_model_update_review_brief.txt, but you don't need to read those to
answer this brief.

The question now on the table
----------------------------------
City, as designed, is implicitly a *high-civilization* settlement — a
place with a town hall, a shop, a blacksmith, an inn, real buildings and
services, matching what a human or elven town looks like. But the world
also has goblins, orcs, wolves, spiders, undead, dragonkin — creatures
that clearly shouldn't get the same kind of settlement a human city gets,
if they get one at all. So: what does "where the monsters live" actually
look like, across a whole range of creatures from a lone predator's den
up to an organized raiding tribe?

What the world's own content already suggests, unprompted
---------------------------------------------------------------
Before designing anything new, the existing race and monster content was
checked directly for clues about how this was already being imagined,
even informally. It turns out a real pattern is already there, just never
pulled together into an actual game mechanic:

- Every race carries a list of natural traits. Humans, elves, and dwarves
  all carry "tool_user" and something equivalent to "social_humanoid" (a
  tag meaning "builds and cooperates like a person, not an animal").
  Goblins and orcs carry "tool_user" too, and goblins specifically carry
  "social_humanoid" as well — but wolves, spiders, and slimes carry
  neither. Trolls and undead don't carry "tool_user" either.
- Every race also lists which social/leadership roles its members can
  hold. Goblins and orcs both include "shaman" and "leader" — real
  organizing/spiritual roles, not just "fighter." Wolves and spiders only
  get "predator_hunter" (or, for a wolf, "alpha" — a pack leader, but not
  a builder or a shaman).
- Monster archetypes carry loose thematic tags for flavor — and a wolf's
  archetype is literally tagged "wolf_den" alongside "forest." That
  concept (a den — a claimed lair, not a built settlement) already exists
  as a name in the content, just not as a real mechanic anywhere.
- Every faction (the game's term for a named power in the world, which
  covers everything from a human kingdom down to a goblin warband) also
  carries a role describing what *kind* of force it is — not just
  "friendly" or "hostile." A human kingdom's ruling council is tagged
  "sovereign." A goblin warband is tagged "challenger." An orc clan and a
  lizardfolk tribe are both tagged "territorial_power." A cult built
  around a single dragon is tagged "boss_pressure" — a completely
  different shape of threat, organized around one powerful individual
  rather than a settlement or a tribe at all. Undead remnants are tagged
  "corruption_pressure" — implying they don't build anything, they
  *spread* through and corrupt something that already existed.
- Separately, ruins already exist as real, explorable, hazardous
  locations distinct from any settlement — one confirmed example is an
  old, haunted battlefield, corrupted by undead, far more dangerous than
  any town. A mine and an underground cavern also exist as their own
  distinct, tagged locations. None of these have any population of their
  own the way a city or a goblin camp would — they're empty, hazardous,
  and clearly meant to be explored and looted, not lived in.

So, without anyone designing it on purpose, the raw content already
implies four genuinely different kinds of "place a hostile or non-human
force calls home" — they've just never been named or built as an actual
system:

1. A proper **City** — tool-using, cooperative, high-civilization
   peoples (human, elf, dwarf). Buildings, services, an economy, real
   citizens.
2. A **Camp** — tool-using, organized-but-lesser peoples (goblin, orc,
   plausibly lizardfolk). A real, growing, dangerous presence with
   *some* structure — the design so far imagines a totem, a stockpile,
   a chieftain's tent, a crude palisade — but nothing close to a city's
   full building roster. It can raid outward as it grows, and an
   adventurer can raid it back and clear it for loot.
3. A **Lair** — no construction at all, just a claimed territory
   anchor for a pack predator or a single dangerous creature (a wolf
   den, a troll's cave, a dragon's mountain). Not a settlement in any
   sense — a place a specific dangerous creature or small group has
   staked out.
4. A **Site** — an empty, hazardous, explorable location with no
   population of its own at all: ruins, a mine, an ancient battlefield.
   Sometimes explicitly a *former* settlement, now abandoned, corrupted,
   or haunted by something that moved in afterward (undead, spirits)
   rather than built the place themselves.

None of this is built yet except the City concept (already real) and a
very early, thin version of "Camp" (a single growing threat number with
no internal structure at all yet, and not currently switched on in any
real playthrough). Lair and Site aren't built as distinct mechanics at
all — Site in particular is currently just an ordinary stretch of land
with a hazard flavor and a tag, no different mechanically from any other
patch of wilderness.

What we want your read on
------------------------------
1. Does this four-way split (City / Camp / Lair / Site) actually match
   how fantasy worldbuilding — books, tabletop RPGs, video games —
   usually handles "where do the monsters live"? Is it missing an
   obvious category, or drawing a line in the wrong place? (For
   instance: should Lair and Camp really be separate, or is that a
   distinction without a difference? Is there a fifth tier we're not
   seeing — something between a war-band's camp and a proper city, or
   below a lone predator's den?)

2. For each of the four tiers, what's the single most important texture
   or trope it needs to actually feel right, that a flat "threat level"
   number wouldn't capture? What makes a reader or player go "yes, that's
   a goblin camp" versus just "that's a slightly weaker city"?

3. Ruins specifically: fantasy fiction leans hard on the idea that a
   ruin has a story — it used to be something. Does it matter, for a
   Site to feel real, whether the world actually knows *what* it used to
   be (a fallen City? a Camp overrun?), or is "it's old and dangerous and
   full of loot" enough on its own?

4. The "boss_pressure" case (a dragon cult, organized around one
   powerful individual rather than a settlement or tribe) doesn't fit
   cleanly into City/Camp/Lair/Site as framed above. Is that its own,
   fifth thing worth naming — a stronghold that exists *because* of one
   powerful creature, rather than a population — or does it fold
   naturally into Lair (just a very dangerous one)?

5. Any classic mechanics or story beats from this genre — a dungeon
   getting more dangerous the deeper you go, a monster camp sending out
   raiding parties that a town has to defend against, a ruin slowly being
   reclaimed by nature or civilization after it's cleared, factions
   fighting over who controls a dangerous Site — that this framing is
   currently missing and should at least consider?

What NOT to do
-----------------
- Don't check anything against a codebase — you don't have one, and this
  isn't that kind of review.
- Don't propose a sprawling new simulation system (economies for monster
  camps, full AI-driven dungeon ecology, etc.) — the project's whole
  approach is to keep each new piece small and reuse what already exists
  rather than invent something big. Suggestions should be additive
  texture on top of the four-tier idea above, not a redesign of it.
- Don't worry about whether any of this is technically feasible to build
  — that's being handled separately. Focus entirely on whether it *feels
  right* for the genre.

How to report back
----------------------
Answer the five questions above directly. If you think the whole framing
is wrong, say so and explain why — this is meant to be a real second
opinion, not a rubber stamp. Close with whichever single addition or
change you think would most improve how authentic this feels, if you can
only pick one.
