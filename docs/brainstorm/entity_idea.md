I treated the revised Character Capabilities page as ground truth. The useful foundation is already broad: characters have stats and needs, personality, occupation, aging, transformation, wounds/scars, relationships, goals, beliefs, limited attention and memory, and several forms of decision-making.  The ideas below therefore focus on **new dimensions of a life**, rather than filling one of the already-recorded gaps.

### 1. Life Stages & Rites of Passage

A character's life could be divided into meaningful stages rather than aging being only a continuous march toward old age. A young character might begin dependent on others, move through a coming-of-age or apprenticeship period, enter an adult occupation, and eventually retire or take on an elder role. Different kinds of characters would move through these stages differently: a wolf might mature rapidly and become independent almost immediately, while a human could spend a substantial part of early life learning a trade, and a long-lived magical being might have stages lasting many times longer. The important part is that age would change **what kind of life is available**, not merely the character's numbers.

**Mainly touches:** Who they are.

**Why it serves the vision:** It gives ordinary people and simpler creatures recognizable life arcs without requiring every character to behave like an open-ended adventurer.

---

### 2. Careers, Apprenticeships & Changing Occupations

Occupation could become something a character enters, leaves, and grows through over a lifetime. A townsperson might work as a laborer, become an apprentice, eventually run a shop, then train someone younger; a Guard might become a mercenary or retire into civilian work; an adventurer injured badly enough might leave the road and take up a safer occupation. Opportunities, relationships, success, wealth, age, and local circumstances could all influence those changes. This is about **a social life path**, rather than simply learning another combat skill.

**Mainly touches:** Who they are.

**Why it serves the vision:** It makes non-adventurers capable of having genuinely different biographies even when they never follow a branching heroic build.

---

### 3. Relationship Roles, Not Just Relationship Scores

Two characters could develop a recognized relationship **type** in addition to liking, trust, familiarity, fear, or rivalry. Examples could include close friend, family member, employer and worker, patron and client, mentor and apprentice, creditor and debtor, dependent and caretaker, sworn companion, or long-term business partner. These roles would carry expectations: a dependent expects protection, an apprentice seeks guidance, an employer expects work, and a close friend may help in situations where a merely well-liked acquaintance would not. Relationships could also change type as the characters' shared history develops.

**Mainly touches:** How they think and remember.

**Why it serves the vision:** A believable world needs relationships with meaning and obligations, not only numerical attitudes between otherwise interchangeable individuals.

---

### 4. Earned Habits & Behavioral Tendencies

Repeated behavior could gradually create habits that sit between fixed personality and deliberate decision-making. A character who repeatedly solves danger by running might become quicker to retreat; someone who repeatedly succeeds through careful preparation might increasingly prepare before taking risks; a character who habitually helps strangers could develop a strong tendency to intervene when others are threatened. Habits would not need to be permanent personality changes: they could strengthen through repetition and weaken when a character consistently behaves differently.

**Mainly touches:** How they think and remember.

**Why it serves the vision:** Two characters who began with similar stats and personality could become meaningfully different because of what they have actually lived through.

---

### 5. Personal Economy & Material Ambition

Characters could have their own relationship with money and possessions rather than wealth mattering only when paying for an immediate action. One character might maintain an emergency reserve, another spend aggressively on better equipment, a Shopkeeper might try to accumulate enough stock or wealth to expand their livelihood, and an ordinary worker might spend years saving toward a personally important purchase. Greed, occupation, goals, dependents, age, and recent hardship could all shape those priorities.

This would allow economic choices to form a visible part of a character's biography: earning, saving, losing wealth, recovering from loss, becoming prosperous, or living perpetually on the edge.

**Mainly touches:** What they do.

**Why it serves the vision:** Economic differences give ordinary characters reasons to interact, cooperate and compete even when neither side is involved in combat or adventure.

---

### 6. Secrets, Confidence & Selective Disclosure

Not everything a character knows would automatically be information they are willing to share. Some knowledge could be treated as private, entrusted by someone else, embarrassing, dangerous, commercially valuable, or politically sensitive. A character would decide whether to reveal it based on who is asking, how much they trust them, what they stand to gain, and what might happen if the secret spreads.

Being trusted with a secret would itself become part of a relationship. Revealing another character's confidence could damage that relationship even if the information was true, while keeping a dangerous secret might create tension between loyalty and self-preservation.

**Mainly touches:** How they think and remember.

**Why it serves the vision:** It turns information into a social resource and creates richer interaction than a world where every known fact is simply available for exchange.

---

### 7. Expertise Earned Through Living

Characters could gradually become recognized as knowledgeable about subjects they repeatedly experience rather than specialists existing only because their starting role says so. A hunter who has spent years dealing with a particular creature might genuinely know more about it. A merchant who travels the same roads could become an authority on local prices and routes. A Guard stationed in one region might know its dangers far better than a newly arrived Hero.

Expertise would therefore be partly **biographical**. Characters could seek out someone because that person has actually accumulated relevant experience, and a former expert could eventually become outdated if the world changes around them.

**Mainly touches:** How they think and remember.

**Why it serves the vision:** It creates natural interdependence—different lives produce different knowledge, giving characters real reasons to need one another.

---

### 8. Learning by Watching & Choosing Role Models

Characters could learn socially without anyone formally teaching them. A character repeatedly watching another person's success could start copying parts of their behavior: a young Guard might adopt the tactics of a respected veteran, a novice adventurer might begin favoring the same kinds of missions as someone they admire, or a simple creature might learn that following a particular hunting pattern reliably leads to food.

The sophistication of imitation could scale with intelligence. A simple animal might reproduce a directly observed action, while a highly intelligent character could infer the principle behind what they saw and adapt it to a different situation.

**Mainly touches:** How they think and remember.

**Why it serves the vision:** It lets intelligence change the *kind* of life-learning a character is capable of, while also allowing behavior to spread organically through interaction.

---

### 9. Creature Territories & Simple Life Cycles

Lower-intelligence creatures could have a real life path built around direct biological or supernatural pressures rather than human-style careers and abstract ambitions. A predator might claim territory, search for food, rest after feeding, defend nesting grounds, compete with rivals, reproduce or spawn replacements, and eventually be displaced by a stronger creature. Pack creatures could behave differently from solitary ones without needing the same rich planning used by intelligent characters.

Magical or demonic beings could eventually have equally simple but very different cycles—for example, drawing strength from particular environments, consuming magical energy, splitting, corrupting another creature, or returning after destruction—without pretending they live like humans.

**Mainly touches:** What they do.

**Why it serves the vision:** It gives simple creatures a genuine existence of their own instead of making them combat encounters waiting for an adventurer to arrive.

---

### 10. Injury That Changes a Life, Not Just a Number

Major injuries could sometimes force a character to adapt rather than merely carry a permanent penalty. A badly injured fighter might switch to a weapon that suits their remaining strengths, avoid certain kinds of combat, depend more heavily on companions, pursue treatment, or eventually leave a dangerous occupation. Another character might adapt successfully and develop a distinctive fighting style around the limitation.

The result would be that two otherwise similar characters could become radically different because one survived something the other never experienced.

**Mainly touches:** How injury and conditions affect them.

**Why it serves the vision:** It turns survival itself into a source of branching life paths and lets a character's body become part of their personal history.

---

### 11. Possessions With Personal History

Items could matter to a character for reasons beyond their combat value or sale price. A weapon used through dozens of victories, a gift from a trusted companion, something taken from a defeated rival, or an item inherited from another character could gradually become personally significant. A character might refuse to sell it, go looking for it if lost, prefer it over a technically better replacement, or pass it to someone important later.

The attachment would belong to the **character-item relationship**, so two identical swords could carry completely different meaning to their owners.

**Mainly touches:** Who they are.

**Why it serves the vision:** It gives characters continuity across events and lets relationships and memories become visible in the physical world around them.

---

### 12. Personal Dependents & Responsibility

Some characters could become responsible for another specific character: an elder caring for someone young, a veteran protecting an inexperienced companion, a wealthy character supporting a dependent, or an injured person temporarily relying on someone else. Responsibility would compete with self-interest. A character with someone depending on them might avoid unnecessary danger, prioritize earning food or money, rush home when threatened, or accept work they otherwise would not take.

The relationship could also reverse over time—a younger dependent eventually becoming the caretaker of an aging character.

**Mainly touches:** What they do.

**Why it serves the vision:** It creates life choices driven by another person's welfare, making characters feel embedded in a society rather than behaving as isolated decision-makers.

---

Of these, I think **Life Stages**, **Changing Occupations**, **Relationship Roles**, **Creature Life Cycles**, **Learning by Watching**, and **Injury-Driven Adaptation** are the most valuable next-level directions. Together they solve a deeper problem than adding more actions: they make a character's **history alter the kind of person or creature they become**.

That is probably the key property I would use when judging future character ideas: not merely *“does this give them another thing to do?”*, but **“can this cause two characters who started similarly to end up living recognizably different lives?”**
