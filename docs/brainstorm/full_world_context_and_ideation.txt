RPG World Context & Open Ideation Request
================================================================================

This is a complete, self-contained handoff. Everything you need is below --
no links to follow, nothing external to fetch. This is a creative-generation
task, not a review of anything. You're being given a fantasy RPG
simulation's full creative direction and an honest, plain-language account
of what it currently delivers, then asked one open question at the very
end: given all this, what would you actually build next?

What kind of RPG this is, in one line: an autonomous, deterministic
simulation -- hundreds of characters exploring, fighting, crafting, and
forming societies entirely on their own, with nobody scripting what
happens. Nobody plays a character in it. You watch a world live, and the
stories that emerge are a side effect of its systems colliding, never
authored in advance. Think "an ant farm meets Final Fantasy."

This document has two parts. Part 1 is the creative direction -- eight
principles describing what this specific world is trying to be, each with
real examples already produced and an explicit description of what failure
looks like. Part 2 is an honest, plain-language account of what the
simulation actually delivers today, organized the same way a player or
observer would think about it -- one character, up through parties,
settlements, nations, peoples, and the world itself. Neither part contains
code, file paths, or implementation detail -- everything is written in
plain language on purpose.

Read both parts. Part 1 especially -- it's the actual compass for judging
whether an idea fits this specific world or is just a generically "cool RPG
feature" that could belong to any fantasy game. The request itself is at
the very end, after both parts.

==============================================================================
PART 1 — THE CREATIVE DIRECTION: THE UNWRITTEN WORLD
==============================================================================

"An RPG world that plays itself — a deterministic, concurrent simulation
engine where hundreds of autonomous entities explore, fight, craft, and form
factions across a living world — while you watch the stories emerge." Think of
it as an ant farm meets Final Fantasy: a living world you observe rather than
control.

The eight governing principles, in short:
1. No one hands down the story — it has to be found.
2. Individuals carry their own trajectory, distinguishable from the
   aggregate.
3. Different creatures should be different, not reskinned humans.
4. No single loyalty wins by default.
5. Places remember what happened to them.
6. Nothing sits still forever.
7. A world that feels lived-in without drowning in bookkeeping.
8. The world has to tell on itself — legible from outside, not just correct
   underneath.

Each one, in depth:

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
No one hands down the story — it has to be found.
[The governing idea — everything else answers to this one]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
If you can point to a single line of authored logic that says "and here is
where the story happens," it isn't real yet. It's staged. A real story in this
world is what's left over after independent characters, each acting on reasons
that make sense only to them, happen to collide.

  [Already true, unplanned]
  A cautious hero avoids a goblin camp for five hundred ticks, grinding weaker
  creatures in the forest instead. Eventually it raids the camp — and finds
  the goblin chief has grown stronger over that same stretch of time. Nobody
  scripted that irony. Two separate growth curves, running on their own
  clocks, happened to meet at the worst possible moment.

The principle should reach further than combat pacing, though. Picture a
border war and a marriage that have nothing to do with each other on paper —
until the war's front line moves and a married couple who were never political
suddenly find themselves living on opposite sides of a new border. Nobody
wrote "star-crossed lovers." A boundary moved for entirely unrelated military
reasons, and a private life got complicated as a side effect. That's the
texture this whole world is reaching for: consequence that leaks sideways
between systems that were never designed to talk to each other.

This changes what a good new idea actually looks like. The right test for any
new mechanic isn't "does this produce a satisfying story on its own" — it's
does this create a new intersection other systems can stumble into. Narrative
satisfaction is what you get afterward, never something you aim at directly.

  [What failure looks like]
  A mechanic that produces the same shape of consequence no matter what else
  is happening around it — a scripted-feeling beat, or a random event with no
  thread connecting it to anything the world already knows about that
  character or that place.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Individuals carry their own trajectory, distinguishable from the aggregate.
[Scale: one character]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The world tracks populations — a region's headcount, a race's numbers —
because it has to; nobody can hand-simulate every wolf. But a name, a face, a
specific history must never quietly dissolve back into that aggregate. Two
entities built from an identical starting template should still visibly
diverge, because of what actually happened to each of them and nothing else.

This is already partly true in a small, honest way: personality is assigned
once, at the moment a character is created, and never changes afterward — so
two otherwise-identical entities are already walking different paths from day
one, before anything has even happened to them yet. The reproduction design
pushes this further on purpose: a child's two parents, their birth city, their
exact place in a lineage are permanently recorded — and stay recorded even
after both parents die. That fact is never folded back into the anonymous
population count the way everyone else's existence is. It stays a fact about
that one child, forever.

  [Worth building toward]
  Two adventurers who started identical should be tellable apart without
  looking at their stat sheets. One has survived three near-deaths and grown
  visibly warier, slower to trust a new ally, quicker to retreat. The other
  has had an easy run and still charges in without thinking. Not "higher
  level" — a genuinely different shape of person, earned through specific,
  rememberable events rather than a difficulty slider.

Hold the tension honestly rather than resolving it: the aggregate isn't the
enemy here. A regional population count is a weather system, and weather
systems are allowed to be abstractions. The failure is only in confusing the
two — treating a specific person as if they were interchangeable with the
average, or letting "the population trends this way" quietly stand in for what
an actual named character would do.

Part of what makes a trajectory someone's own is that it isn't just handed to
them. A character is never merely acted upon — whatever happens to them,
joining a group, marrying, swearing new allegiance, still has to make sense as
something they, specifically, agreed to or resisted, given who they are, not
something imposed wholesale by whoever initiated it. Every time this world has
had to decide whether a relationship forms silently or through a real
decision, the real decision has been the right answer: a recruitment offer a
target can refuse based on their own trust and history, a marriage proposal
either side is genuinely free to decline. An entity that only ever has things
happen to it isn't really an individual in this world's terms, no matter how
distinct its stats look.

  [What failure looks like]
  Any moment where a named, historied character's behavior is
  indistinguishable from what the regional average would predict — where
  knowing their specific history would never have changed the outcome. Or,
  just as much a failure of this principle: a relationship or commitment that
  simply happens to a character, with no point at which their own reasons
  could have refused it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Different creatures should be different, not reskinned humans.
[Scale: species and kind]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A goblin camp, a wolf's den, and a dwarven city need to feel like
fundamentally different kinds of life — not the same settlement shape wearing
three different costumes with three different numbers attached.

This already shows up as a real distinction, not just a hoped-for one: some
creatures build and cooperate the way people do; some hunt in coordinated
packs but never build anything at all; some are barely more than a spreading
population with no leader in it whatsoever. A goblin camp, a wolf's den, and a
spider nest aren't three tiers of the same idea — they're three different
kinds of answer to "how does this creature exist in the world."

The distinction should reach past settlement shape into how a creature relates
to its own life. A magical or demonic being doesn't have a childhood at all —
it manifests already at full capability, the moment it comes into existence.
That isn't a numbers difference from a human's slow climb through youth; it's
an entirely different relationship to time itself. A dragon's life isn't a
coming-of-age story with bigger stakes. It's not that kind of story to begin
with.

  [Worth pushing on]
  Creature psychology should diverge the same way settlement shape does. A
  wolf pack's loyalty is territorial instinct — it has nothing to do with an
  oath. A goblin's loyalty to its chief is opportunistic, tied to whoever's
  currently winning. A human's loyalty to a guild can survive the guild
  losing, because it's not instinct or opportunism, it's something closer to
  identity. Giving every creature the same "trust" number, just tuned to
  different thresholds, would quietly undo this whole principle even while
  looking correct on a spreadsheet.

  [What failure looks like]
  A bestiary where every entry is functionally a human with different numbers
  and a different sprite — same drives, same social shape, same emotional
  register, only the paint job differs.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
No single loyalty wins by default.
[Scale: relationships and politics]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A person can love someone their nation is at war with. A guild can pull a
member against their own king. The world should let that tension sit there,
unresolved, rather than quietly picking a winner on the character's behalf.
The friction is the content — smoothing it away would be deleting the
interesting part, not fixing a bug.

  [Already true, worth protecting]
  A human Guard, loyal to a Country at war with a human Shopkeeper's Country,
  who nonetheless loves that Shopkeeper personally. Neither loyalty erases the
  other. A trading company with members living in cities on both sides of that
  same war, whose entire reason to exist is not taking a side — an
  organization defined by the conflict its own members are personally caught
  inside of.

The more interesting version of this principle isn't the dramatic one where a
character has to make a big, visible choice between two loyalties. It's the
quiet version: most conflicting loyalties in an actual life never erupt into
open crisis. They just create small frictions — a moment's hesitation, a
decision that's slightly worse than it should have been, a private grudge
nobody else ever hears about — that accumulate over a long life without ever
resolving into a single dramatic scene. A dwarf whose own Clan profits from a
war their Country is losing probably doesn't have a breakdown about it. They
probably just carry it, unevenly, for years.

The craft implication follows directly: don't design a system whose job is to
resolve a loyalty conflict into one answer. Design each loyalty to push on its
own terms, independently, and let a character's actual behavior land wherever
those pushes happen to add up — even when that's a messy, half-contradictory
middle that no single loyalty would have chosen on its own.

  [What failure looks like]
  Any system where one layer of loyalty (political, always, is the usual
  culprit) silently overrides every other one whenever they'd conflict — so
  that "who do you love" and "who are you loyal to" always resolve to the same
  answer, every time, for every character.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Places remember what happened to them.
[Scale: geography and history]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A ruin shouldn't just be "the ruins." It should be legible as a specific
fallen city, a razed war-camp, a den something else moved into after its
original owner died. The scars on the map are the world's own memory of itself
— readable by anyone who knows how to look, the way a real landscape carries
the marks of what happened on it.

  [The strongest single idea surfaced so far]
  Goblins establish a camp beside an old battlefield. They grow, and start
  raiding a nearby city. Heroes destroy the camp. Nobody occupies it
  afterward. Years later, giant spiders have settled into the abandoned
  structures, and the place is now remembered as a spider-infested ruin. No
  quest was authored for any of it. Today's world becomes tomorrow's
  archaeology — a place's identity can change entirely, as a direct record of
  what actually happened there, not as a separately-spawned "ruin" with no
  relationship to its own past.

The principle shouldn't stop at physical structures. Political geography
deserves the same memory: a border that's moved five times over the life of a
long-running world should leave some visible trace behind, not just its
current position. A city that's changed hands between two warring powers three
times should feel different from one that's always quietly belonged to the
same crown — contested, uneasy, its loyalties still not fully settled even
generations later.

And the memory should run in both directions. It isn't only that places
remember events — characters should remember places. A hero who grew up in a
city that later fell should carry that loss forward as something specific and
real: grief tied to an actual location and its actual history, not an abstract
sadness with no address.

  [What failure looks like]
  Any place whose entire state can be fully described as "type plus a hazard
  number" — indistinguishable from a freshly-generated location that happens
  to share the same stats, no matter what actually happened there.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Nothing sits still forever.
[Scale: pacing and pressure]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Left alone, a threat should get worse, not wait patiently to be dealt with
whenever it's convenient. A grudge should fester. An ambition should grow. A
quiet, static world is a dead one — the sense that things are always moving
toward something, with or without anyone pushing them, is what makes a world
worth continuing to watch.

  [Already true, worth extending]
  A goblin camp's danger climbs the longer it's ignored, until it's dangerous
  enough to raid outward on its own — "you should have dealt with this
  earlier" is the whole feeling in one sentence. The idea worth pursuing
  further: population pressure building in a crowded city feeds a nation's own
  ambition to expand, which leads to new places being founded or fought over,
  which draws population into them in turn, which builds pressure somewhere
  else. No layer's resting state is actually a rest — everything is either
  growing, decaying, or straining toward a change.

This shouldn't be limited to territory and threat. Relationships deserve the
same restlessness. An unaddressed grudge shouldn't sit at a fixed value
forever, waiting to be triggered — it should be able to fade, or curdle, on
its own. A trust score that's never tested by anything shouldn't stay
perfectly, permanently trusted by default; an ally you haven't spoken to in
years probably isn't owed the same confidence as one you saw yesterday. This
is a genuinely open thread, not something already decided — but it follows the
same logic as everything already built: ambient drift, not just reaction to
direct events.

  [What failure looks like]
  Any system whose entire state is a single number that only ever moves when
  something directly acts on it — genuinely static, indefinitely, the moment
  nobody's paying attention to it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A world that feels lived-in without drowning in bookkeeping.
[Scale: how much detail actually earns its keep]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the discipline that keeps the other six from spiraling into simulating
everything for its own sake. Enough weight that a wound, a marriage, a death
actually costs something real — without needing to track the underlying
biology or logistics behind any of it. Depth should be chosen for what it
produces, not for how realistic it sounds on paper.

The reproduction design draws this line about as cleanly as it's been drawn
anywhere: real parentage, a real birth event, a real requirement that a child
grow up before becoming an adult — and, deliberately, no gestation tracking,
no fertility stats, no courtship simulation underneath any of it. Those
weren't left out by accident or by running out of time. They were left out
because they wouldn't have paid off — nobody watching this world would ever
notice the difference between a simulated pregnancy and an instant one, but
everybody would notice a fourteen-year-old suddenly commanding an army.

The honest test for any future idea is simple to state and hard to apply
consistently: if this were fully built, would anyone watching ever actually
notice the difference? A wound's severity changing someone's combat odds —
noticed, every time. A fully modeled digestive system — never noticed by
anyone, ever, ranked against the actual production of a watchable story.

  [The cautionary tale, already real]
  Somewhere in this world right now sit several genuinely well-designed, fully
  typed systems — how a character understands its own strengths and
  weaknesses, a richer private-trust model, more than one competing memory
  system — that were built carefully and then never actually switched on or
  fed real data. They're precise. They're invisible. They're load-bearing for
  nothing. That's this principle's failure mode taken to its natural end:
  depth that never surfaces to anyone might as well not exist — and worse, it
  creates the appearance of richness that was never actually delivered. Prefer
  one coarse signal an observer can actually feel over three precise ones
  nobody will ever see.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The world has to tell on itself — legible from outside, not just correct underneath.
[Scale: how it reads to whoever's watching — cuts across every scale above it]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Emergence that nobody can follow is indistinguishable from noise. The founding
pitch's own phrase is "a living world you observe" — and observing requires
something to actually notice. A rise, a fall, a rivalry, a betrayal has to be
recognizable as it happens, not just true, in principle, somewhere inside the
simulation's internal state.

  [Already built for exactly this reason]
  The world writes its own history automatically — reviewing what happened,
  picking out which events were actually significant, and producing a real,
  readable account of them, with nobody authoring it by hand. That system
  exists because "technically emergent" and "actually noticeable" are two
  different claims, and only one of them is worth anything to an observer.

Reputation and notoriety do the same job one level down, for a single
character instead of the whole world. They're not just internal bookkeeping —
they're the mechanism by which one person's history becomes visible to others
in the world, not merely true in a record only the engine itself can see. A
hero nobody's heard of and a hero whose name makes people flinch might be
mechanically similar underneath. Only one of those is a story anyone else in
the world could actually tell.

This principle should push on every other one from a specific angle: not just
"don't build depth nobody notices" (that's the bookkeeping principle's job),
but actively ask, for anything new, how would this actually become visible —
to a watching observer, or to another character inside the world? A place
remembering what happened to it only pays off if that memory can surface
somehow: in its name, its terrain, a rumor a passing traveler picks up. A
memory that never becomes visible to anyone is a memory in name only.

  [What failure looks like]
  Any true, correctly-computed fact about the world with no path to ever
  becoming visible — not to an outside observer, not to another character, not
  even to the world's own auto-generated history. A fact that can never be
  told might as well not have happened.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT THIS DIRECTION IS FOR
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
What this document is for This isn't a technical specification and it isn't a
status report — those already exist, and they're both explicitly about what's
built versus what isn't. This is the compass: the thing a new idea should be
checked against before asking whether it's feasible to build at all. The
intended next step is a wide, unconstrained brainstorming pass — collecting as
many RPG ideas as possible, including from an outside perspective specifically
because anyone who's been reasoning inside this project for a long stretch
(human or otherwise) stops being a reliable source of genuinely fresh angles.
That pass needs to stay anchored to what makes this world's identity
distinctive, rather than drifting toward generic RPG ideas that would fit any
fantasy game equally well. This document is that anchor. An idea worth
pursuing should visibly serve at least one of the eight principles above; an
idea that doesn't — no matter how clever on its own terms — probably belongs
to a different project.


==============================================================================
PART 2 — WHAT THIS SIMULATION ALREADY DELIVERS
==============================================================================

A plain-language, honest status of what the simulation currently does, from a
single character's choices up through parties, cities, nations, peoples, and
the living world. Every item below is tagged with one of three states:
  LIVE     - working in the simulation today
  BUILT    - fully built, correct, but currently switched off or never triggered
  PLANNED  - designed, at least in outline, but not yet built at all

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT CHARACTERS DO
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Fighting, moving, and interacting with the world — everything a character
actively does, moment to moment.

* Combat & Tactics  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Fighting is one of the most developed parts of the simulation. Before
  landing a hit, the engine weighs five factors: high ground, flanking, being
  surrounded, taking cover, and fighting alongside a trusted, familiar ally.
  Defense counts roughly twice as heavily as attack when the two clash. Armor
  and weapons wear down with use, slot by slot, and grow less effective as
  they take damage — fully broken gear contributes nothing until repaired.
  Area attacks exclude allied characters from their affected targets. A hero
  who dies isn't necessarily gone for good — they can be reborn to fight
  again, up to four times, before a combat death can become permanent.

* In-the-Moment Fight Decisions  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Beyond deciding whether to fight, characters make real tactical choices
  moment by moment. Target selection weighs trust in allies, recent momentum
  (it won't flip-flop between two targets erratically), and battlefield
  pressure. A wounded character, or one exposed to ranged attacks, will
  actively seek cover; front-line fighters can coordinate with allies to
  attack a target from opposing sides, and a dedicated protector role actively
  shields the wounded. Characters can intercept a moving target's path or hold
  a defensive position, and won't chase an enemy so far from their group that
  they end up isolated and overwhelmed. A locked-in objective won't be
  abandoned mid-fight on a whim, but a character caught in a losing fight can
  still be swept up by a danger/retreat response and break off an attack
  rather than fighting to the bitter end — this is a simpler, always-on
  reflex, distinct from the richer named-emotion model described later, which
  doesn't currently run. Characters also won't get stuck endlessly repeating
  the same failed move against a stubborn opponent — after ten stale moments
  with no progress, they'll try something else.

* Sizing Up a Fight Before It Starts  [BUILT — Built, not yet active]
  A system exists for a character to weigh up an opponent and themselves —
  assessing perceived strength, estimating their own chances, and picking a
  stance — before committing to a fight at all, deciding whether it's a fight
  worth having in the first place. It's fully built and ready, but currently
  switched off in the running simulation.

* Moving Around the World  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Movement is a real, weight- and speed-aware system, not a simple coordinate
  update. The heavier a character is loaded down and the lower their agility,
  the more of their action-readiness moving costs — the same readiness meter
  described below that governs how often a character gets to act, so a slow,
  overloaded character effectively spends more of their turn just getting
  somewhere. Two characters trying to swap positions at the exact same moment
  are handled correctly: one moves, the other waits its turn, and they trade
  places safely rather than colliding.

* How Often a Character Gets to Act  [LIVE — Live, but not yet varied]
  Every character has to fully charge up (reach 100% readiness) before they
  get to act again, so in principle a faster character should act more often
  than a slow one. Right now, though, every character charges up at exactly
  the same fixed rate regardless of their stats — a swift character and a
  sluggish one currently act equally often.

* Gathering, Looting & Opening Chests  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Gathering resources, looting the fallen, and opening chests all take a
  moment to complete. Progress resets if the character moves, switches to a
  different target mid-action, or takes a hit worth more than roughly 5% of
  their max health — "you got hit while foraging, you stop." The engine also
  checks there's actually room to carry what's found before letting the action
  finish, and an opened chest can't be used again until a waiting period has
  passed.

* Paying for Information  [BUILT — Built, but never actually triggers]
  A system was designed for characters to pay other characters for
  information. The transaction logic itself is real and does run its check
  every moment — but it needs two specific things to ever actually fire: a
  character with an active "seeking information" goal, and another character
  formally registered as a recognized information source. Checked closely:
  neither of those two things is ever produced anywhere in the simulation
  today, in any world. So the check runs, finds nothing to act on, and moves
  on — a transaction has never once actually completed.

* Trading or Gifting Directly Between Characters  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  Two characters can't currently hand items to each other directly. The only
  way items move between people today is indirectly, by each side separately
  buying from or selling to a shop.

* Asking Someone to Team Up  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  A character can't currently ask another character to team up and have them
  accept or decline. Groups are assembled automatically behind the scenes
  today, based on how well characters would fit together, rather than through
  an actual invitation offered by one character and accepted by another.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHO EACH CHARACTER IS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Stats, race, personality, and how a character changes over a lifetime of play.
A quick note on terms used across this section: race/archetype sets a
character's starting template; class governs their long-term stat and skill
progression; occupation is their day-to-day social/job role; combat role
describes their battlefield behavior; and doctrine is a class-linked play-
style bias. They're six genuinely distinct things, not different names for the
same idea.

* Core Stats & Basic Needs  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Every character has nine core stats — Strength, Agility, Vitality,
  Endurance, Intelligence, Spirit, Wisdom, Perception, and Charisma — plus
  three basic needs: hunger, sleep, and stamina. Hunger and sleep debt each
  build up steadily and only reset when the character actually eats or rests,
  never fading away on their own. Left unaddressed, hunger past roughly 90 out
  of 100 starts causing real starvation damage, and sleep debt past roughly 95
  causes exhaustion damage.

* How Stats Become Real Numbers  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  A character's raw stats are converted into the numbers that actually matter
  in play: Vitality and Endurance build health, Strength drives attack,
  Vitality also grounds defense, and Agility improves the chance to dodge.
  Equipment, skills, and traits each layer their own bonus on top of the base
  numbers. A character's natural battlefield role — front-line fighter,
  skirmisher, or protector — is picked from whichever of Strength, Agility, or
  Vitality is highest, with a 5-point buffer built in so the role doesn't
  flicker back and forth from small stat changes. One notable design fact:
  this combat-number calculation and the separate mental-bandwidth calculation
  described later both start from the same core stats, but are two entirely
  independent calculations that don't reference each other. One notable
  absence: nothing today makes a sharper-eyed character actually see or detect
  further — no vision or detection range is currently derived from a
  character's Perception stat.

* Race & Starting Template  [LIVE — Live, and foundational]
  A character's race and archetype ("goblin warrior," "human ranger," and so
  on) is the real starting point for almost everything else about them,
  composed from five separate building blocks: a base-stat template, a
  starting-skill list, a set of core drives, a thinking-and-planning style,
  and a combat profile. Race genuinely shapes a character from the ground up —
  it also factors into how a character judges who's a threat and picks combat
  targets, not just their starting numbers. One small content gap found: a few
  starting-skill lists reference skills that don't actually exist in the game
  yet — a fireball-like skill, a backstab-style strike, an aimed shot, a
  defensive stance, an appraisal skill, and a repair skill are all referenced
  but not implemented — leftover references to older content.

* Personality  [LIVE — Live, well-integrated]
  Every character is given a real, consistent personality when they're
  created, measured on four traits — greed, bravery, sociability, and
  industriousness — each assigned once and never changing afterward. It
  genuinely shapes behavior: braver characters get a real shift toward more
  aggressive combat play and hold their ground in moments that would make a
  more cautious character retreat. Sociability has its own concrete effect —
  when a party needs a new leader, the most sociable member takes over as long
  as they're meaningfully more sociable than the current leader, not just
  marginally so.

* Different Paths to Power  [PLANNED — Mostly not yet built]
  Two characters of the same class and race grow along an identical numeric
  path today: the same flat stat-point grant every level, the same three fixed
  skill unlocks, the same formulas throughout, from level 1 all the way to the
  level cap. They differ only in how far along that path they've gotten, not
  in which path they took. Worth being precise about why: characters do
  receive stat points to spend each level, but the action that would actually
  let a character choose where to spend them is never triggered by anyone in a
  live simulation today — so in practice, no character's stat points ever
  diverge from any other's, even though the spending mechanism itself
  technically exists. There's one real exception to "identical path":
  adventurer-type characters do pursue their own evolving sequence of longer-
  term goals — upgrade gear, chase a quest, gather resources — which genuinely
  diverges between individuals even though the underlying numbers stay
  identical. A true branching, choose-your-own-build system doesn't exist yet.

* Aging & Old-Age Death  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Every character ages continuously and can die of old age at a fixed lifespan
  (around 10,000 in-game moments), separate from dying in combat. Elderly
  characters — roughly the last 30% of that lifespan — do already need
  noticeably more sleep than younger ones, about 50% more, a real and
  currently live effect of growing old.

* Inheritance  [BUILT — Built, never triggers]
  A real system exists for a dying character's belongings and treasured items
  to pass to a chosen heir. But nothing in the simulation currently ever
  actually chooses an heir for anyone — so this inheritance system, while
  built correctly, has never once triggered in practice.

* Elder Frailty (Age-Based Stat Changes)  [BUILT — Built, never applied]
  A system exists that, if switched on, would make elderly characters roughly
  30% weaker in Strength and Agility and 50% weaker in Vitality and Endurance,
  while boosting Wisdom and Charisma by about 30% — wiser but frailer. It's
  never actually applied today, so characters die of old age without first
  growing mechanically weaker in this way (though see Aging & Old-Age Death
  for the one elder effect that is live).

* Genetic Variation  [BUILT — Built, but never applied]
  A real system exists for giving each character a subtly different genetic
  multiplier on every stat — anywhere from an 80% to a 130% version of the
  norm, a genuine natural head start or handicap. It's never actually turned
  on, though; every character currently gets the same flat, neutral value for
  every stat instead. There's also no birth or reproduction event anywhere in
  the simulation today — see Family, Marriage & Growing Up for the design that
  would change that.

* Growing Into a Stronger Form  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Every character has an evolution chain tied to their race, checked at levels
  10, 25, and 50 as successive checkpoints. Named races have their own
  specific transformation (a goblin becomes a goblin warrior, then later an
  orc scout; a wolf becomes a dire wolf), and this isn't a monster-only
  mechanic — heroes can transform into a legendary form too, through the exact
  same underlying system. Any race without a specifically designed chain still
  gets a generic fallback transformation, so this isn't limited to a hand-
  picked subset of races. Currently this only ever depends on experience —
  nothing about what a character has found or carries factors in. A planned
  addition would let some races transform down a different path if the
  character has found and is carrying a specific rare material when they'd
  otherwise transform — an alternate outcome layered on top of the existing
  one, not a replacement for it.

* Learning New Skills  [LIVE — Live, but limited]
  Characters unlock a small, fixed set of new skills as they level up —
  currently three: a power strike at level 2, swifter reflexes at level 5, and
  a fireball at level 10. Real, but a short list rather than a deep skill
  tree. Separately, a character's starting skills come from their race and
  archetype, not from leveling up.

* Experience & Leveling  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Characters earn experience and level up in a real, working system. Four base
  classes exist today — Novice, Warrior, Mage, and Rogue — each with its own
  starting health, attack, and defense. The level cap is 99, and the
  experience needed for the next level grows steeply as a character advances.
  Every level grants a flat amount of stat points to spend, regardless of what
  the character actually did to earn it.

* Practice-Based Combat Experience  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  A narrower, real form of practice-based mastery already exists: characters
  build up genuine combat rank over time simply by fighting, which makes them
  measurably better fighters. It's a much smaller system than a full mastery
  tree, but it is live and working today.

* Deeper Mastery & Breakthroughs  [BUILT — Built, but does nothing]
  A deeper "mastery through practice" and "breakthrough" system — permanent
  bonuses earned by pushing a stat past a milestone — was designed but was
  never finished. It exists in the simulation but currently does nothing when
  checked. Separately, a character can pay gold to formally learn a skill at a
  class hall — a real, working action, distinct from the unfinished
  breakthrough system — but nothing currently chooses to use it.

* Occupation & Social Role  [LIVE — Live, and one of the richest systems found]
  Every character has a real, active occupation — Hero, Shopkeeper, Monster,
  Townsperson, Laborer, or Guard — and it genuinely changes how they behave:
  what they spend their day doing, what they're able to craft, how movement
  conflicts between characters get resolved (Heroes get double the tie-break
  priority of Monsters), and even whether they can be permanently killed
  outright in combat (Heroes specifically cannot). Occupation also changes how
  a character responds when their nation becomes involved in a larger conflict
  — a Guard responds more urgently to a call to defend a border, a Shopkeeper
  to a trade opportunity, a Hero to a commissioned quest.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INJURY & PHYSICAL CONDITIONS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
How injury marks a character, and what temporary conditions currently exist.
This section covers what's built today rather than every form of lasting
change — leveling, aging, and transformation, which also change a character
permanently over time, are covered in the previous section.

* Wounds & Scars  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Any single hit costing a character more than roughly 25% of their maximum
  health leaves a lasting Wound. A Wound's penalty scales with how severe the
  hit was — a glancing wound costs little, while the most severe wounds can
  cost a character up to about 3 points of attack, 2 of defense, 2 of speed,
  and 10 points of max health, all at once. Once a Wound heals, it may leave
  behind a smaller Permanent Scar that keeps about 30% of the original penalty
  — forever. This is the simulation's clearest example of injury having a
  lasting, meaningful consequence, though the exact odds of a scar forming,
  and what specifically triggers a wound to heal, aren't pinned down with a
  stable number yet.

* Temporary Conditions (Buffs, Debuffs, Freezing & More)  [LIVE — A few real effects are live; no unified system yet]
  A handful of real conditions exist and genuinely affect characters right
  now: exhaustion measurably reduces combat power, hunger and lack of sleep
  both cut into mental sharpness and bandwidth, and a "Frozen" state exists
  that stops a character from acting. But there's no single, unified system
  for temporary conditions yet — each one is currently its own small, separate
  mechanism rather than one shared system. Skill-granted effects, like a
  temporary speed boost from a spell, don't exist today.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
HOW CHARACTERS THINK & REMEMBER
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Perception, memory, emotion, relationships, and the decision-making that
drives everything above. A note on time: this page uses "moment" for the
simulation's basic unit of time — one update cycle for the world, not a fixed
real-world duration.

* How a Character Sees Itself  [BUILT — Built, not yet active]
  A rich system exists for characters to form their own subjective sense of
  their strengths, weaknesses, and confidence — separate from the objective
  truth — including an awareness of things they know they don't know. Facts a
  character believes about itself gradually lose certainty over a very long
  stretch of time (roughly 10,000 moments) if never reconfirmed. It's fully
  built and correct, but currently switched off in the running simulation.

* A Second, Larger Mental Model  [BUILT — Built, but almost entirely empty]
  An entire second, even richer structure for a character's inner mental life
  is built — covering risk perception, recovering from near-death experiences,
  a private trust score toward each individual other character on top of a
  separate public reputation, and a seven-part motivation profile (survival,
  reward, knowledge, loyalty, pride, curiosity, and caution). The structure
  itself is real, but almost none of it is actually populated with real data
  yet.

* What a Character Notices  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Characters don't perceive everything happening around them — attention is
  genuinely limited to the ten most relevant things happening around a
  character at once, with irrelevant details dropped entirely and the most
  relevant surfaced first, driven by what the character currently needs. A
  real form of fog-of-war. That cap of ten is currently fixed for every
  character alike, not yet scaled up for a more perceptive one.

* Emotions  [BUILT — Fully designed, never active]
  A well-designed emotional system exists, modeling seven distinct emotional
  responses — fear, confidence, frustration, curiosity, satisfaction, panic,
  and boredom — each responding to real events: a near-death moment spikes
  fear and panic while dropping confidence, an easy win builds confidence and
  satisfaction, repeated failure builds frustration, and a long stretch of
  nothing happening builds boredom. It's never actually switched on anywhere,
  though — characters don't currently feel any of this in a running
  simulation. (The simpler danger/retreat response described under In-the-
  Moment Fight Decisions is a different, always-on mechanism, not this one.)

* Relationships & Affection  [LIVE — Live and rich, with a couple of gaps]
  Characters track a real, bounded affection score toward specific other
  characters — ranging from -1 (deeply disliked) to +1 (deeply liked) —
  alongside a separate familiarity score that builds from 0 to 1 the more two
  characters interact, plus trust, grudges, and fear. It genuinely affects
  behavior: allies who like and trust each other fight better together, and a
  proposed cooperation agreement between two characters is refused outright
  once trust drops below roughly 0.2 or liking drops below roughly -0.8. A
  character who's caused serious harm three separate times gets classified as
  a recognized rival. Two gaps remain: there's no in-between step where two
  characters need to build up enough rapport before an interaction like a
  conversation can even start, and affection toward a place, like a home city,
  is tracked but doesn't influence anything yet.

* What Drives a Character  [LIVE — Live for play-style bias, inert for deeper values]
  Characters have real underlying motivations, tied to their class, that bias
  the choices they make — three named play-styles exist today (warrior,
  ranger, and mage doctrines), each with its own preferred and avoided
  activities and its own bias for or against teaming up, plus a default
  fallback for anything else. Worth noting: this doesn't map perfectly onto
  the four actual classes — there's a ranger doctrine with no matching ranger
  class, while two classes (Novice and Rogue) currently fall back to the
  default doctrine rather than having one of their own. A further layer of
  value-driven bias — like valuing safety over curiosity — exists in the
  simulation but currently has no real data feeding it, so it has no actual
  effect yet.

* Keeping Promises & Recognizing Betrayal  [LIVE — Mostly live, one final step missing]
  The simulation tracks the growing pressure of an approaching deadline a
  character has committed to, with that pressure ramping up sharply in the
  last twenty moments before it hits — a specific, live mechanism distinct
  from the general sense-of-time system described next, which isn't active. It
  also classifies how a character abandons a group: abandoning for genuine
  survival reasons carries no penalty, a greedy desertion carries a heavy
  trust penalty, and a more sympathetic voluntary quit carries a lighter one —
  and known betrayers are weighted twice as heavily against when a character
  is choosing who to work with next. One piece is still missing: the final
  step that would turn a witnessed betrayal into an actual public reputation
  label doesn't run yet.

* Sense of Time & Deadlines  [BUILT — Minimal, not currently active]
  Separate from the live commitment-deadline pressure described above, a very
  small, general-purpose system exists for characters to feel urgency about
  any approaching deadline, but it isn't currently active, and nothing beyond
  that basic urgency is built out yet. No calendar, seasons, or day/night
  cycle exists in this part of the simulation.

* Goals, Leads & Obstacles  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Characters maintain a real chain of intent, from a broad goal down to a
  concrete action, and track leads — things they've learned about the world,
  which gradually become less reliable if not refreshed — alongside six
  recognized kinds of obstacle standing in their way: lacking access, lacking
  materials, lacking inventory space, lacking the right capability or skill, a
  social obstacle, or a group-related obstacle.

* Rumors, Beliefs & Being Proven Wrong  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Characters treat rumors as less certain than things they've witnessed
  firsthand, and a belief can be gradually undermined by repeated contrary
  evidence — each new piece of contrary information chips away at both the
  original tip and the belief it fed, with the system keeping count of how
  many times it's been contradicted. This is the mechanism that lets a false
  rumor be believed at first and slowly fall apart as it's disproven.

* Asking Specific People for Information  [BUILT — Built, not yet active]
  A separate, richer system from Paying for Information above: a character can
  seek out the right kind of specialist for a specific question — a guide for
  directions, a blacksmith for a recipe — pay for the answer, and update how
  much they trust that source afterward. Confirmed-accurate information raises
  trust in that source by a small amount, partially-confirmed information by
  less, contradicted information cuts trust substantially, and information
  later proven to be a deliberate lie cuts trust by roughly a quarter in a
  single stroke — the steepest penalty in the system. It only kicks in when a
  character genuinely has a specific unanswered question, never as idle
  chatter. It's currently switched off in the running simulation. A related,
  planned idea (see Nations & Politics) would let two people who trust each
  other enough simply talk and share what they each know, not just pay a
  specialist for a formal answer — including passing along a piece of news the
  way a real rumor spreads, getting a little less reliable each time it
  changes hands.

* Short-Term Leads & Limited Capacity  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  The version of "limited memory" that's actually live today: leads (see
  Goals, Leads & Obstacles) are capped to a limited number held at once, using
  four named certainty levels — precise, approximate, vague, and exhausted —
  rather than a precise percentage. That cap itself isn't a fixed number for
  every character; it scales up or down the same way the limits described in
  Mental Bandwidth & Exhaustion do, so sharper characters can hold onto more
  at once.

* Percentage-Based Trust Memory  [BUILT — Built, not yet active]
  A more precise version of memory trustfulness exists, using an actual
  percentage rather than the four rough levels described above. It correctly
  fades in trustworthiness over time — roughly halving over about 10,000
  moments if never refreshed — but is currently switched off.

* Short-Term vs. Long-Term Memory Storage  [BUILT — Built, never runs]
  A third, fully separate approach to memory exists: a proper short-term store
  that discards its oldest items to make room for new ones, alongside a
  separate long-term store that only ever accumulates and never forgets. It's
  fully built but never runs at all — none of these three memory approaches
  are aware of the other two.

* Remembering Why Something Went Wrong  [BUILT — Built, never runs]
  A system exists for characters to work out why an attempt failed,
  recognizing four specific causes — lost a fight, a search came up empty, a
  crafting attempt failed, or a group fell apart — each logged at a fixed
  confidence level of 0.8 (on a 0-to-1 scale), and to build up region-by-
  region familiarity and danger awareness from that experience over time.
  Nothing in the running simulation currently triggers it, though, so this
  kind of learning-from-experience never actually happens today.

* Choosing What to Do Next  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Characters choose from a genuinely wide range of activities — 15 distinct
  kinds in total today, well beyond a simple
  fight/gather/explore/escort/travel/investigate/build list, including things
  like deliberately falling back to regroup with a party or standing down from
  a bad situation entirely.

* Big-Picture Decision Making  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Characters combine their current goals, immediate needs, group obligations,
  known information, and current situation to decide which objective to pursue
  next. This is the main decision layer connecting what a character knows and
  wants to what they actually do — it's what turns everything described
  elsewhere on this page (perception, memory, relationships, motivation) into
  an actual choice of what to do.

* Planning Several Steps Ahead  [BUILT — Built for reading, nothing writes to it yet]
  A system for a character to hold a short sequence of upcoming actions —
  train, then craft, then go on a quest, up to three steps queued at once —
  exists and can be read from. But nothing in the simulation currently writes
  a real multi-step plan into it, so in practice no character has one yet.

* Mental Bandwidth & Exhaustion  [LIVE — Live, well-integrated]
  Smarter, wiser characters can genuinely juggle more at once: how many goals
  a character can hold scales with the average of their Intelligence and
  Wisdom, how many leads they can track scales with Perception, and how many
  concerns they can hold scales with Wisdom. Being hungry or sleep-deprived
  measurably shrinks all of that mental bandwidth — every limit is cut in half
  once hunger or sleep debt crosses roughly 70 out of 100, and cut by about a
  fifth once it crosses 40.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FAMILY, MARRIAGE & GROWING UP
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
None of this exists in the simulation yet, but it's a fully worked-out design,
not a vague wish list — included here because it's some of the highest-value
planned work and touches almost everything else about a character's life.

* Different Kinds of Life  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  The design splits every living thing into three fundamentally different
  categories rather than treating them all the same way. Wild creatures
  (wolves, spiders, and the like) would keep reproducing the fast, parentless
  way they already do today — a territory simply produces a new one from time
  to time. Ordinary people (human and humanoid) would get a real birth event
  with real parents. Magical and demonic beings would come into existence
  through a ritual, a disaster, or a corrupted place, not through two parents
  at all.

* Being Born  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  For ordinary people, a birth would require both parents to be alive, grown,
  married, in the same place, and past a cooling-off period since their last
  child. No pregnancy, fertility, or complications would be simulated — birth
  would simply happen the moment all the requirements are met. A newborn's
  traits would genuinely take after both parents rather than being random: two
  battle-hardened adventurer parents would nudge a child's potential toward
  combat-ready traits, while two settled, everyday parents would nudge toward
  a more ordinary spread. It's explicitly accepted as fine, even desirable,
  for a family or a whole settlement to eventually decline and die out for
  good — nothing would force population to bounce back automatically.

* Marriage  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  Two characters would be able to propose marriage to each other, with the
  other side genuinely free to accept or decline — the first idea anywhere in
  the whole design for a real two-sided "yes or no" exchange between two
  characters. Marriage would be required for two people of the same peaceful,
  humanoid kind to have children, but there's no restriction at all on their
  job or role — a Hero could freely marry a Shopkeeper, or a Guard a
  Townsperson. The only requirement is that both belong to the same race.

* Growing Up  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  A child born this way wouldn't be handed a finished adult identity at birth
  the way every character is today. They'd go through a real childhood first,
  and only decide what kind of adult they become — what class, what path —
  once they actually come of age. That choice would lean toward whatever their
  parents did for a living and toward what their home settlement currently
  needs, but never be locked to it outright — a soft nudge shaped by
  upbringing and personality, not a rule that removes the choice entirely.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WORKING TOGETHER: PARTIES
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
How characters band together into groups, and what happens when that falls
apart.

* Forming a Party  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Characters do form real, working parties — but it isn't a simple auto-
  assembly. It starts with one character making a real recruitment offer to
  another, and the other side genuinely judges whether to accept, based on how
  much they trust the person asking: their personal history together, or that
  person's general standing if they've never met. An offer from someone with a
  history of betrayal, or someone the target simply distrusts enough, gets
  refused outright. Only once an offer is accepted, and the two are actually
  close enough together, does a real party form. Parties made of well-matched,
  complementary characters genuinely fight better together than a mismatched
  group.

* Party Leadership & Falling Apart  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  A party's leadership can change hands peacefully: if another member is
  clearly more sociable and well-liked than the current leader, not just
  marginally so, they take over. But if the actual leader dies or otherwise
  disappears, the party doesn't get a replacement leader at all — it disbands
  on the spot. Members who leave for genuine, sympathetic reasons face no real
  penalty; a greedy, self-serving desertion does lasting damage to how much
  others trust that character afterward.

* Guild Halls  [LIVE — Live, but modest]
  Characters can visit a guild hall for guild-flavored services in one
  specific place. It's real and working, but modest — a guild today is closer
  to a single building offering a service than an organization with its own
  members and holdings spread across more than one place (see Organizations
  Beyond Borders).

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CITIES & SETTLEMENTS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Towns, buildings, and the services they offer. A city in this simulation is a
real, bounded place with its own buildings and population, not just a label on
the map.

* Towns as Real Places  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  A settlement is a real, bounded place with its own buildings and population,
  and more than one can exist at once in the same world under different
  ownership — confirmed directly in a real, already-built scenario with two
  separate towns, each with its own buildings, residents, and owner, sitting
  near each other with contested wild territory in between. A settlement can
  be fought over and its ownership can change hands.

* Buildings & Town Services  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  A working set of town buildings exists, each offering a real service: a town
  hall, a shop, a blacksmith for repairs, an inn for rest, a healer's hut, a
  class hall for training, and a church offering blessings and resurrection.
  This is a genuinely built-out town-services system, not a single generic
  "building" type.

* Sabotaging Buildings  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  A character can deliberately target a town building and damage it, wearing
  down its health over time through repeated sabotage — a real, working
  mechanic for undermining a settlement from the inside, not just attacking it
  from the outside.

* Knowing You're 'Home'  [BUILT — Confirmed not working correctly]
  Several systems — a character returning home to rest, recover, or restock —
  rely on the game correctly recognizing when a character has actually reached
  a settlement. Checked directly and confirmed this doesn't work reliably: in
  a world with more than one town, the game can lose track of exactly where
  "home" is, so characters who should be recognized as back in a settlement
  sometimes aren't. A real, confirmed problem, not a design gap.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE WIDER LAND
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The countryside and wilderness a settlement sits in — its own scale, separate
from any one town.

* Contested Territory & War Damage  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Land carries a real, visible scar of trauma and hazard from what's happened
  there, and control over a stretch of land can genuinely shift back and forth
  based on the fighting that takes place in it. A heavily-fought-over stretch
  of land stays visibly marked by that fighting.

* Population Ebb & Flow  [PLANNED — Built, but confirmed not working]
  The design calls for regions to have a real, living population that rises
  and falls on its own — birth and death rates, and people migrating away from
  an overcrowded or dangerous area toward somewhere better off. It's built,
  and its internal math is correct when tested in isolation — but checked
  directly and confirmed that no real, actually-running world ever gives it a
  starting population to work with, so in every real game today this never
  actually happens. Population figures at this larger scale simply never move.
  Once this is actually working, the plan is for that same population pressure
  to be what actually drives a crowded nation's ambition to expand (see A
  Nation's Own Ambition) and what pushes new goblin camps or monster nests to
  spring up in unclaimed wilderness nearby — turning three separate ideas into
  one real cycle: population grows, pressure builds, someone responds to it,
  new places get claimed or founded, and population grows into them in turn.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
GOBLIN CAMPS, MONSTER DENS & RUINS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Everything from a proper city down to a lone monster's den — what a home looks
like for creatures that aren't human, elf, or dwarf, and the empty, dangerous
places worth exploring in between.

* Goblin & Orc Camps  [PLANNED — Built, but confirmed not working]
  A real system exists for a goblin or orc camp to grow over time, spawn more
  of its kind the longer it's left alone, and eventually launch a real raid
  against the nearest town — with real rewards for an adventurer who clears it
  out first. Checked directly, and confirmed this never actually happens in
  any game today: nothing ever creates a starting camp for the system to grow
  from, so the whole growth-and-raid cycle sits idle every single time, even
  though the logic behind it is genuinely built and correct.

* Ruins, Mines & Old Battlefields  [LIVE — Live, but not yet its own kind of place]
  Explorable, hazardous locations distinct from any settlement already exist —
  a haunted, undead-corrupted battlefield and an old mine are both real,
  working examples — and a quest can genuinely require going to one of these
  specific kinds of place. What doesn't exist yet is anything that makes a
  ruin different from ordinary wild land beyond a label: no population of its
  own, and nothing that distinguishes it mechanically from any other patch of
  wilderness unless something else has since moved in.

* A Different Kind of Home for Different Creatures  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  Right now, only humans, elves, and dwarves would ever get a proper
  settlement (see Cities & Settlements). The plan going forward is
  deliberately not all-or-nothing: goblins and orcs are organized enough to
  have a real camp of their own — cruder than a city, with a leader and a
  little crude structure, but nowhere near a full town — while something like
  a wolf or a spider isn't a builder at all and would never have a settlement
  in that sense, only a claimed patch of territory.

* Nests & Swarms  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  Some creatures — spiders, slimes, and similar — aren't organized enough to
  have a leader or build anything at all, but can still exist in real numbers
  spreading outward from one source. The plan treats that as its own thing,
  distinct from a goblin camp: nobody plans a raid, because there's no leader
  to plan one, but a growing nest can still spread and become a real,
  worsening problem if nobody deals with it.

* A Monster's Own Den  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  A single dangerous creature — a wolf, a troll, a dragon — could claim its
  own territory without building anything at all. The idea is that a den like
  this is defined by whoever lives there: kill the dragon, and the den itself
  fundamentally changes, unlike a goblin camp, which keeps going even after
  any one specific goblin dies.

* Places That Change Over Time  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  The most ambitious of these ideas: a place's identity could genuinely change
  as a result of what actually happens in the world, instead of staying fixed
  forever. A city destroyed in a war could become a ruin. An abandoned goblin
  camp could later be taken over by something else entirely. A cleared-out
  ruin could faintly remember what it used to be — "this used to be a dwarven
  mining camp" — without needing a full, hand-written history behind every
  location.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
NATIONS & POLITICS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The political powers that own settlements and land, and how they relate to
each other and to individual characters.

* Diplomacy  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Nations have a real relationship status toward each other — neutral, tense,
  hostile, or at war — that genuinely shifts over time based on what happens
  between them, backed by real diplomatic actions a nation can take.

* War, Sieges & Exhaustion  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Betrayal between nations, siege warfare against a settlement, and a nation
  growing exhausted from prolonged war are all real, working parts of the
  simulation.

* Agreements Between Characters  [LIVE — Live, substantial]
  Characters can enter real formal agreements with each other — recruitment,
  loans, protection, trade, even a simple request to swap positions — each
  judged and either accepted or refused by the other side, not automatic. This
  is one of the largest, most developed social systems in the simulation.

* Reputation  [LIVE — Live, richer than it first looks]
  Every character has a real public reputation, plus separate heroism and
  notoriety scores that track their standing in very different directions.
  Alongside that public standing, characters also track a private trust and
  familiarity toward each specific other character they've dealt with — a
  public face and a private one, both real.

* The World Remembers  [LIVE — Live, with one narrative piece still missing]
  What characters witness is genuinely remembered and carried forward. A
  nation can hold a grudge as a whole even after the specific individual
  responsible is gone. When a close ally dies, the survivor carries real,
  lingering grief into what comes next. A character who's betrayed the same
  person repeatedly, across more than one chapter of play, creates a lasting,
  recognized refusal to ever work with them again. The one piece still
  missing: turning a witnessed, remembered event into an actual written story
  beat — a legendary arrival, a infamous betrayal becoming public knowledge —
  is built but doesn't run yet, so the underlying memory is real even though
  it doesn't yet surface as a narrative moment.

* Nations Can't Be Founded or Destroyed  [PLANNED — Confirmed not built]
  Checked directly: the full list of nations in a world is fixed from the
  moment that world is created and never changes for the rest of the game. A
  nation that loses every settlement and all its strength doesn't actually
  cease to exist — it just sits there, permanently, with nothing left. No new
  nation can ever split off or be founded during play, either. Everything else
  about a nation genuinely changes over time (see the cards above) — it's
  specifically the two extremes, being founded and being wiped out, that don't
  exist.

* A Nation's Own Ambition  [LIVE — Live, but capped at three responses]
  A nation already reconsiders its own priorities every moment, based on its
  real situation — a tense nation with real territory moves to defend its
  border, a strong and calm one turns toward trade, and any nation holding
  territory at all puts out real quests. That's a genuine, working form of
  national ambition, not something imagined. What it can't do yet is grow:
  there's no fourth option for a strong, confident nation to actually expand
  into new territory — see Nations Can't Be Founded or Destroyed. Adding that
  growth option is planned, and would be the natural way a nation's territory
  could change hands because it chose to grow, not only as a side effect of
  battles fought near its border.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ORGANIZATIONS BEYOND BORDERS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A group defined by who belongs to it, rather than by what land it controls — a
guild, a bloodline, a religious order, a trading company with members and
property in more than one city or even more than one nation.

* Organizations Beyond Borders  [PLANNED — Confirmed not built]
  Checked directly for anything resembling this, and it's genuinely absent —
  not a thin version of the idea, but nothing at all. There's no way today for
  a group to have members and property spread across more than one settlement
  or nation, independent of who currently owns the land those members happen
  to be standing on. Worth noting: two of the real nations in the game today
  are literally named things like "orc clan" — but mechanically, they're just
  ordinary nations with a clan-flavored name, no different underneath from any
  other nation on the map.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PEOPLES & RACES
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Race as a living, changing population — a different question from what race
one specific character belongs to.

* A Character's Own Race  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Every individual character has a real race, and it genuinely matters — it's
  part of their starting template (see Who Each Character Is) and it factors
  into how that character personally judges who's a friend and who's a threat.

* Race as a Population  [PLANNED — Confirmed not built]
  Checked directly for anything tracking race at the level of an entire
  people, and found nothing. There's currently no way to ask whether the elf
  population across the world is growing or shrinking, and no general sense of
  how one people tends to regard another as a whole, separate from any one
  individual's personal opinion. A planned idea would let a general elf-vs-
  dwarf (or any pairing) baseline attitude factor into how strangers size each
  other up on first meeting — on top of, not instead of, how any two specific
  characters actually feel about each other personally.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE LIVING WORLD
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Systems that operate above any one character, party, or nation — the world's
own ongoing story and events.

* Ongoing Campaigns  [LIVE — Live, the single largest system in the simulation]
  Play can span multiple connected chapters, with experience, levels, gear,
  reputation, and a character's overall life story genuinely carrying forward
  from one chapter into the next. Characters and nations that were wiped out
  in one chapter correctly stay wiped out when the next one begins, rather
  than being quietly reset.

* Automatic History Book  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  The simulation writes its own history automatically — reviewing what
  happened, picking out the significant events, and producing a real written
  account of them, without anyone having to author it by hand.

* Rumors & Quest Hooks Bubbling Up From the World  [BUILT — Built correctly, switched off by default]
  A real system exists for the world itself to generate quest hooks and rumors
  organically from what's happening, rather than every quest coming from a
  fixed source — genuinely substantial and correctly built, but currently
  switched off in every real run today.

* Cultures Slowly Changing  [LIVE — Live, modest]
  A population's culture can shift gradually over a long stretch of play. Real
  and working, though a relatively small, contained system rather than a
  sprawling one.

* Disasters, Monster Bosses & Threats  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  A region under enough combined strain and damage can spawn a powerful boss
  monster, trigger a raid from a hostile nation, or see its local threats grow
  stronger over time — a real escalation system tied to how battered a place
  has become.

* Building New Worlds  [LIVE — Live, a content-creation tool]
  New worlds and scenarios are assembled from reusable, declarative building
  blocks rather than hand-placed one at a time, with real checks in place to
  catch a broken or inconsistent world before it's ever played.

* Gods & Religion  [PLANNED — Confirmed not built]
  Checked directly, and there's no religious system of any kind today. The
  only trace anywhere is a single button-label at a church building offering a
  generic "blessing" — one word, not an actual mechanic behind it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
MEMORY, REPUTATION & LEGACY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
None of this exists in the simulation yet, but it's a real, thought-through
set of ideas, not a wish list — reputation, feuds, and history that outlive
the specific person who earned them.

* A Reputation Before You've Earned One  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  A newborn character doesn't get a completely blank reputation the way they
  do today. The plan is for them to start carrying a small echo of their
  parents' public standing instead. A child of a celebrated hero would be
  trusted a little faster; a child of someone notorious would start a step
  behind, having to actively earn back what their parent lost. That echo would
  fade on its own as the child's own actions build a reputation that's
  genuinely theirs.

* An Organization's Own Name  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  Once organizations spanning more than one city exist (see Organizations
  Beyond Borders), the plan is for the organization itself to carry its own
  reputation, separate from any single member's. One member's real betrayal
  could drag the whole group's standing down — meaning every other member,
  even ones who did nothing wrong, would start new relationships a step
  behind, purely because of who they belong to.

* A Feud That Outlives Everyone Who Started It  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  A grudge that survives the death of the person who earned it, passed down to
  their children or chosen heir the way a name is. Combined with the real
  marriage system already planned, this creates something nobody would have
  scripted: two people who've done nothing to each other, falling for one
  another across a conflict that actually belongs to their parents.

* A City That Drifts Away Without a Single Event  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  A settlement cut off from its capital for long enough — by distance, by a
  siege, or just by years of little contact — could see its people's felt
  loyalty erode purely from growing apart culturally, with no rebellion, no
  vote, no single moment anyone could point to. Not a city being conquered or
  seized. A population that simply stops feeling connected to a place it
  shares less and less with.

* When History Inspires the Next Hero  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  The world already writes its own history automatically (see The Living
  World), but right now that history is only a record — nothing in the world
  actually reacts to it. The plan is for a hero's fame, once it's real enough,
  to become something other characters can genuinely hear about and be moved
  by: a young person choosing to become an adventurer specifically because
  they grew up hearing about a real, specific hero's deeds.

* A Final Wish That Shapes What Comes Next  [PLANNED — Not yet built]
  When a character dies and leaves an heir, the plan is for their last
  specific wish — avenge me, protect your sibling, finish what I started — to
  become a real, lasting pull on that heir's own motivations, separate from
  anything inherited by blood or by will. The heir could honor it, ignore it,
  or actively turn away from it — but it would be there, shaping their choices
  either way.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TRADE, GEAR & CRAFTING
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The economy: what characters carry, buy, sell, and make.

* Gear & Auto-Equip  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Characters genuinely evaluate their equipment, weigh up whether a new item
  is actually an upgrade, and automatically switch to better gear when it's
  worth it — a real decision process, not a simple "pick the biggest number"
  rule.

* Trading, Storage & Resource Limits  [LIVE — Live in the simulation]
  Buying, selling, harvesting, and home storage are all real and working,
  governed by strict rules that prevent items or gold from ever being
  accidentally duplicated or lost in a transaction.

* Crafting  [LIVE — Real system, very little to craft]
  Crafting itself is a genuine, properly-gated system — a character has to
  have actually learned a recipe first, needs the right role and skill, and
  needs the materials, gold, and carrying space to complete it. What's thin is
  the content: only three actual recipes exist in the game today. A richer set
  of rare, exotic materials tied to specific places already exists behind the
  scenes but isn't reachable through crafting yet — the plan is to connect the
  two, so a specific material found only in a specific kind of ruin or biome
  becomes a real reason to go there, rather than exploring only ever paying
  off as combat loot.

THE REQUEST
================================================================================

Given everything above: what would you build next?

Propose as many ideas as you genuinely believe in -- one exceptional idea
is worth more than ten mediocre ones, but there's no target count either
way. Any scale is fair game: one character, a settlement, a wider region,
a nation, an organization spanning borders, an entire people, or the world
itself -- including an idea that starts small and ripples outward into
something bigger.

Don't self-censor for overlap with anything you imagine this project might
already be considering. You weren't given an exclusion list on purpose --
the point of asking is to get a genuinely independent read, not ideas
shaped by trying to guess or avoid an internal roadmap you can't see.
Propose whatever you actually believe in. If something you propose turns
out to already be under consideration, that's still useful information --
say so if you happen to notice, but it is not your job to check.

For each idea, give:
- A short name.
- A paragraph describing what it is and what it would actually change
  about how the world behaves or what kinds of stories it can produce.
- Which of the eight principles from Part 1 it serves, if that's clear to
  you -- and if it doesn't clearly serve any of them, say plainly why you
  think it's still worth considering.

Plain language throughout, the same register as everything above -- no
code, no implementation detail, no file paths. Describe each idea the way
you'd describe it to someone who will never open the source code, because
that's true of everyone who will read your answer.
